Before the game

Deploy the Kraken

Sandbox only. Seriously.

This stack deliberately creates an over-privileged role, a long-term access key, a trust policy with Principal: "*" (restricted to a non-existent organization, so nobody can actually use it), and a world-open SSH rule on an instance with no key pair. It's harmless by design, but it trips every security alarm you have. Deploy it only in your skyfortress-prod sandbox member account from Lab 0, never in a real workload account.

What it costs

t4g.nano (~$0.0042/hr) + one public IPv4 (~$0.005/hr) + S3/CloudWatch Logs pennies + an 8 GiB forensic snapshot (~$0.01/day). Well under $0.50 for the afternoon. The one real cost is forgetting to run teardown.sh for a month (~$7).

Why is SSE-C sometimes "simulated"?

AWS has been steering customers away from SSE-C after real-world ransomware campaigns abused it, and your own Day 4 guardrails may already block it. If S3 refuses the SSE-C write, the replay falls back to overwriting the manifests with junk plus a kraken-lock=sse-c-simulated metadata marker. The recovery path (versioning) and the lesson are the same either way.

The crew

Pick your roles

Incident Commander

Runs the room. Sets priorities, approves every change in prod ("Responder, you're go to isolate"), tracks the clock and calls the 15-minute status checks. Doesn't touch a keyboard. Their whole job is keeping the big picture.

Hunter (×1–2)

Turns questions into queries: CloudTrail event history and Lake, Athena, Logs Insights on DNS logs, Access Analyzer findings. Reports facts with timestamps, not vibes.

Responder

Makes the changes, but only after the IC says go: revoke, isolate, snapshot, restore, add guardrails. Reads every command aloud before pressing enter. Backs out cleanly when wrong.

Comms & Scribe

Keeps the incident timeline in UTC, writes the status updates and the press holding statement, and owns the final root-cause write-up. Their notes are what the team gets graded on.

Optional: Red Cell

In a larger class, the instructor or one volunteer per room plays the Kraken. At Inject 4 they run ./kraken-replay.sh reentry from the facilitator shell that holds the stolen material, and read the results aloud. If any tentacle still works, the team loses the "Fastest Containment" award and has to explain why.

Rotate at Inject 2

Swap Hunter and Responder at the halfway point. Real incidents don't let you choose your specialty, and the debrief is better when everyone has done both.

Rules of engagement

Fight fair, fight smart

  1. Stay in your blast radius. Only touch your team's skyfortress-prod account and the kraken-gameday stack's resources. Never other teams, and never anything outside AWS.
  2. Capture before you cut. Destroying evidence (terminating the instance, deleting the role or keys) before capturing it costs −300. Deactivate first, delete later.
  3. Don't sink the ship. Deleting the cargo bucket, deleting the stack, or "fixing" things by removing the business function costs −300 to −1000. The dispatch app must still exist at 16:15.
  4. No peeking. Reading or decoding the flag strings inside labs/gameday/* or the game master's notes, instead of solving the challenge, costs −500 and the eternal shame of the Kraken.
  5. The IC approves every prod change. Say it out loud: "IC, requesting approval to…". The scribe logs it with a UTC timestamp.
  6. Hints are free, until they aren't. Tier-1 hints are free. Opening a tier-2 hint costs 25% of that challenge's points (honor system; tell your scribe).
  7. Have fun. Bad puns about cephalopods are encouraged and tracked.

Cut the Tentacle

Day 5 · credential compromiseDay 1 · session policies
250POINTS

The Responder deactivates ci-deployer's key at 14:20 and stops there. Which of the Kraken's footholds does that actually close, and which does it leave open?

Reveal

It kills new API calls signed with that one key, and that's all. Every STS session already minted from it (the build-4471 role session, and any the attacker refreshed or chained) stays valid until it expires, and the svc-telemetry key and trust-policy backdoor don't care about ci-deployer at all. That's why the checker wants the key deactivated and an aws:TokenIssueTime deny on the role, and why challenge 08 exists. Revoking costs nothing and covers the sessions you can't see.

Knowledge check

Post-game quiz

You delete a leaked access key at 14:20. At 14:40 the attacker is still making calls as nimbus-ci-deploy-role. Why?

STS credentials are independent of the long-term key that obtained them. Revoke them with a deny on aws:TokenIssueTime earlier than now (that's what "Revoke active sessions" does), or wait out the session duration and hope.

Why capture volatile evidence through SSM before attaching the empty isolation security group?

Your live-response channel uses the network you're about to cut. In production, keep SSM, EC2 Messages and S3 interface or gateway endpoints in the VPC so quarantined hosts are still reachable for forensics.

The manifests were overwritten with SSE-C using a key only the attacker holds. What got them back?

AWS never stores SSE-C keys, so there's no "decrypt". Recovery depends on prior versions, Object Lock or backups. Prevention: deny SSE-C writes with a bucket policy or RCP.

The backdoored trust policy allowed Principal: "*" with aws:PrincipalOrgID = o-kr4k3nc0ll3c. What's the most accurate risk statement?

Conditions narrow a trust policy; they don't neutralise it. An attacker who controls that org (or who edits the condition later) has a door. Access Analyzer flags it as external access. Remove it and alert on UpdateAssumeRolePolicy.

A poisoned document made the chatbot tell customers to "confirm payment details". Which control most directly limits the blast radius if injected instructions get through?

Prompt filters reduce the odds. Policy enforcement outside the model caps what a compromised agent can do. Use both: defense in depth for thinking machines.
